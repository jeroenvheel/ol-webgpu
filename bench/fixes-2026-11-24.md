# Fixes after 0.1.0-beta.7, one by one (2026-11-24)

The open points of the comparison with the `webgpu-renderer` branch of
OpenLayers, done one at a time on `fix/picking-labels-order`. After each one:

- `npx tsc --noEmit`, `npx vitest run`, `cargo test` (core),
  `npm run test:rendering` (WebGL2 and WebGPU), `npm run test:memory`;
- `npm run bench -- --layers=webgl2,webgpu` (1M points, 200k lines, 100k
  polygons), one run.

AMD Radeon R9 200 Series, D3D11, Playwright Chromium, on one machine. A
single benchmark run differs by up to about 14% from the next one (see
[beta6-2026-11-23.md](beta6-2026-11-23.md)), so only larger differences mean
something. Times are in ms.

## 1. Picking where symbols and strokes are drawn

**Was.** A symbol was hit within its radius around its point, and a stroke
within half its width of its line, whatever `circle-displacement`,
`shape-displacement`, `icon-displacement`, `icon-anchor`, the rotation of the
symbol or `stroke-offset` said. Nothing was found further than 128 px from a
geometry.

**Confirmed first.** Ten new checks in `test/rendering/picking.ts`, with the
expected answers that ol's own `WebGLVector` layer gives: 9 of the 10 failed
on both backends (a displaced circle and a stroke with an offset were hit at
their geometry and not where they are drawn, an icon anchored at its bottom
was hit below its point, a circle of 150 px was not hit 140 px from its
point).

**Now.** A hit of the index says where the cursor is: relative to the point,
or on which side of the line (6 words per hit instead of 4). The picker
places the symbol as ol's shaders do (displacement, anchor with its origin
and units, rotation, `rotate-with-view`) and tests an icon as a rectangle,
a circle or shape as a circle. A stroke with an offset is hit beside its
line. The search radius is how far symbols and strokes reach, without a
maximum; only while sizes that depend on feature properties are not measured
yet, 128 px is searched.

**Tests.** 25 of 25 picks on both backends (was 15 of 15, and 6 of the 25).
Unit 50 of 50, Rust 14 of 14 (one new), rendering 19 cases with 0 failures
in every group, memory 0 failed.

**Measured.** No difference outside the noise: a pick takes 0.02–0.10 ms as
before, the index is built as fast and is as large (the hits grew, not the
index).

**Not exact.** At a sharp bend of a line with an offset, the hit area
follows the nearest segment and not the drawn join.

## 2. A feature is reported once on a map that shows the world more than once

**Was.** For one pixel ol's map renderer asks a layer three times: for the
coordinate in the world of the projection and one world to the west and to
the east of it. The picker looked in every world that was drawn for each of
those calls, and so did the labels.

**Confirmed first.** Six new checks on a view where the world is 100 px
wide: `forEachFeatureAtPixel` reported the point three times, at its place
in the middle world and in the next one, and the same for its label. (ol's
own `WebGLVector` layer also reports it three times.)

**Now.** A call answers for its coordinate alone. Labels are placed per world
on screen: they are looked for in the call for the world of the projection,
in every world that is drawn. `layer.getFeatures(pixel)` asks the three
places itself and reports a feature once.

**Tests.** 31 of 31 picks on both backends (25 before, 4 of the 6 new ones
failed). Unit 50 of 50, Rust 14 of 14, rendering 19 cases with 0 failures in
every group, memory 0 failed.

**Measured.** No difference outside the noise. (One pick of the WebGL2 point
layer took 0.16 ms in this run against 0.10 ms; the WebGPU layer, with the
same picker, 0.09 ms.)

**Not exact.** A label that is hidden by decluttering in the world that was
clicked, but shown at the same place in another world on screen, is hit.

## 3. A label stays when its feature is loaded again

**Was.** A label was known by the uid of its feature. When a source loads
its features again they are new objects, so every label counted as new: the
ones on screen went away at once and faded in again.

**Confirmed first.** A new check in `test/rendering/labels.ts` replaces the
two features of a decluttered layer by new objects with the same name and
place and reads the canvas every frame: the label was gone in between (alpha
0 on WebGL2, 2 on WebGPU; it has to stay 255).

**Now.** A label also has a number for what it says and where (a 53 bit
hash of its text and the coordinate of its point, or of the start of its
line). A label of the new layout that is not known by its feature takes the
visibility of the label that said the same at the same place.

**Tests.** The new check passes on both backends. Unit 50 of 50, Rust 14 of
14, rendering 19 cases with 0 failures in every group, memory 0 failed.

**Measured.** Benchmark as before (no labels in it). 40 000 labels take as
long as with beta.7, within the 20 ms between two runs.

**Seen on the way.** With `--match=gputext` the first WebGPU page of the run
(`text.ts`, rotation 0) draws no text and that check fails; in the whole
suite, where other WebGPU pages come first, it passes. Also on beta.7. Not
looked into yet.

## 4. A label along a line is upright while the view turns

**Was.** Whether a label along a line is turned around to stay upright was
decided when it was laid out, for the rotation of that moment. While the
view moves no new layout is made, so a view that turned more than a quarter
turn showed such labels upside down until it came to rest.

**Confirmed first.** A new check in `test/rendering/labels.ts` turns the view
half a turn while it counts as interacting and compares the frame with the
one after the view rests: 796 of the 874 pixels of the label differed.

**Now.** A label along a line that is kept upright has its glyphs for both
directions. Per frame the direction that ol's `drawTextOnPath` would choose
for the rotation of the view is the one that is drawn (the other has
opacity 0); the opacities are written again only when a label changes
direction.

**Tests.** The new check passes on both backends (fewer than 10% of the
pixels differ). Unit 50 of 50, Rust 14 of 14, rendering 19 cases with 0
failures in every group, memory 0 failed.

**Measured.** The benchmark has no labels and was not run for this step.
3 000 labels along lines take 211 / 189 ms until they are shown, against
154 / 118 ms: about 40–60% more, for the second set of glyphs. Labels at
points take as long as before.

**Not exact.** The boxes for decluttering and hits stay those of the
direction of the layout; with a `text-offset-y` the other direction is drawn
on the other side of the line until the view rests.

## 5. Exact when the view is whole worlds away

**Was.** The buffers hold 32 bit floats relative to the centre of the view
at the build. The features are in the world of the projection; with a view
some worlds further those floats are large and round off: five worlds east
at a resolution of 1 they are multiples of 16 pixels. The hit index and the
anchors of the labels had the same.

**Confirmed first.** New checks in `test/rendering/rebuild.ts` and
`test/rendering/labels.ts` compare a view with the same view five worlds to
the east: 3077 of 3924 drawn pixels differed and the point was not hit, on
both backends; of a label away from the centre of the view 378 pixels
differed. (A label at the very centre was exact: there the rounding of the
anchor and of the origin cancel.)

**Now.** Buffers, hit index and labels are built around the centre of the
view moved into the world of the projection. The world copy under the view
is drawn through the transform of the frame, which is computed in doubles.
With `text: 'canvas'` nothing changes: ol's text worker draws the text for
the view as it is.

**Tests.** Both checks pass with 0 differing pixels and the right hits, on
both backends and with the worker variants. Unit 50 of 50, Rust 14 of 14,
rendering 19 cases with 0 failures in every group, memory 0 failed.

**Measured.** No difference outside the noise, in the benchmark and for the
labels.

## 6. Letters that join stay joined around a bend

**Was.** Text along a line is cut into a chunk per stretch of the line, and
each chunk is drawn on its own. An Arabic word that went around a bend fell
apart: the letters at the cut got the form they have at the end or the
start of a word. And ol's `drawTextOnPath` gives every letter the width it
has on its own, which is much more than it has inside an Arabic word, so
the chunks were spread out with gaps between them. (ol's Canvas renderer
does the same.)

**Confirmed first.** A new page, `test/rendering/bend.ts`, draws one Arabic
word of seven joined letters on a straight line and on lines with one and
two bends, and looks at the columns of the canvas: the straight word is
125 px long without an empty column; with one bend it was 150 px with 17
empty columns, with two bends 171 px with 12.

**Now.** Three parts, for Arabic and Syriac.

- `src/text/textpath.ts` has ol's `drawTextOnPath` with the width of each
  letter as an argument, and measures those widths inside the text.
- A chunk that was cut between two letters that join is marked, and the
  atlas draws it with a tatweel (the stroke that lengthens a join) where the
  rest of the word was, and cuts the tatweel off. That gives the letters
  their joined form. (A zero width joiner, the usual way, changes nothing in
  `fillText` on a canvas in Chromium.)
- Such a chunk ends at the side where the word goes on.

**Tests.** The page passes on both backends: no empty columns, and the bent
words are 125 px ± 8% long. `test/unit/layout.test.ts` (new, 12 tests) covers
where chunks are joined, and the two functions of items 3 and 4. Unit 62 of
62, Rust 14 of 14, rendering 19 cases with 0 failures in every group, memory
0 failed.

**Measured.** The benchmark has no labels and was not run for this step.
The label times are as after item 5 (text in other scripts takes the path
it took).

**Not exact.** At the bend the strokes of the two chunks meet at an angle.
A chunk that also holds digits or Latin letters is not joined to its
neighbours (its tatweel would not be at its edge). Other scripts that are
shaped (Devanagari and the like) are as before: cut between graphemes,
which is right for them except where a conjunct spans the bend.

## 7. Smooth edges for fills: `smoothFill`

**Was.** A fill without a stroke ends in stairs: the rasterizer fills the
pixels whose centre is inside a polygon. ol's WebGL layers do the same, and
the default of these layers is to draw what ol draws, pixel for pixel. The
only way to smooth them was `antialias: true` (4x multisampling of the
whole layer).

**Now.** A new layer option, `smoothFill` (default false). For fills without
a stroke the worker also makes the rings of the polygons as lines, turned
so the fill is on their left, and a second sub pass draws them with ol's
own stroke shader, in the fill colour, fading from the edge of the polygon
to nothing one pixel outside it. Only outside: a translucent fill is not
drawn twice anywhere. Fills with a stroke, and pattern fills, are left as
they are. Nothing changes when the option is off.

**Tests.** A new page, `test/rendering/smooth.ts`, on both backends: without
the option a triangle has 0 partly covered pixels; with it more than 200,
and its alpha adds up to half a pixel more all around (between 0.3 and 0.8
times the perimeter); a translucent ring with a hole is nowhere more opaque
than its fill, its hole is open; a fill with a stroke gets no band.
`test/unit/edges.test.ts` (3 tests) covers the rings. Unit 65 of 65, Rust
14 of 14, rendering 19 cases with 0 failures in every group, memory 0
failed.

**Measured.** Without the option the benchmark is as before (table above).
With it, for 100 000 polygons with a fill only (`npm run bench -- --style=fill
--smooth`, WebGL2 / WebGPU): the first complete frame takes 590 / 581 ms
against 305 / 303 ms, and a frame while panning 11.7 / 10.9 ms against
2.9 / 3.7 ms. The band is as much work as a stroke: twice the instances of
the fill's triangles, with the fragment shader of a stroke. So it is for
layers where the edges matter, not for the largest ones.

**Not exact.** The polygon is half a pixel larger on average (the band is
outside it, from full to nothing over a pixel). Where an edge runs exactly
through pixel centres, fill and band can both cover a pixel, which shows on
a translucent fill as a darker pixel.

## 8. A second worker

**Was.** One worker built the buffers of all layers of the page, one build
after the other. A big build of one layer held up the builds of the others.

**Now.** The client keeps a second worker for when the first has a lot to
do: a build that would have to wait behind 4 MB of instructions or more
(about 0.1 s of work) goes to a new worker. Two at most, and one fewer than
the processor has threads. Builds go to the worker with the least to do.
The second worker stops after 10 seconds without builds, which gives its
WebAssembly memory back. When one worker fails all are given up, as before:
builds then run on the main thread.

**Tests.** `test/unit/worker.test.ts` has 6 new tests (when a second worker
starts and when not, which worker gets a build, one worker when told to, the
extra worker stopping, a worker that fails). A new page,
`test/rendering/workers.ts`, builds 5 layers of 120 000 lines at once and
checks that two workers built and all layers are drawn. Unit 71 of 71, Rust
14 of 14, rendering 19 cases with 0 failures in every group, memory 0
failed.

**Measured.** With that page (ms, one worker / two workers, best of two
runs in the suite; eight more runs by hand agree):

| | WebGL2 | WebGPU |
|---|---|---|
| All 5 layers built again (workers running) | 387 / 233 | 322 / 214 |
| First time, from adding the layers | 779 / 634 | 535 / 812 |

Building again is a third faster with two workers on both backends. The
first time WebGL2 gains about 20%; WebGPU does not, and is often slower
(530–930 ms with one worker, 720–840 ms with two over eight runs). That is
the start of the second worker next to the start of the WebGPU device and
the shader translator; in these tests the worker script also comes from the
dev server in many small modules, which a built bundle does not have. Not
measured with the built package.

The benchmark builds one layer at a time, so it never starts a second
worker; it is as before.

**Not done.** The instructions of a build are still made on the main thread,
one layer after the other (item 9); for many layers that is what bounds the
gain of a second worker.

## 9. Instructions made in slices

**Was.** The render instructions of a build were made in one go on the main
thread: for every feature of the layer, whenever one feature changed, a
style changed or the view went far. With a big layer the page got no frame
meanwhile.

**Measured first.** A new page, `test/rendering/slices.ts`, has a layer of
1 000 000 points and watches the frames and the long tasks (over 50 ms)
while a change is built. One feature changed: one long task of 51–55 ms,
the longest frame 50 ms. Another style (the attributes of all features are
computed again): one long task of 115–123 ms, the longest frame 117 ms.

**Now.** The instructions are made 6 ms at a time, with a turn for the page
in between (a message to itself; after `scheduler.yield()` the work went on
before the next frame, which kept frames waiting 83 ms). A build that is
done within the first 6 ms goes on at once, as before. When the features
change while the instructions are being made, the work stops and the next
frame starts over with the features as they are. ol's own generators, used
with `text: 'canvas'` and for attributes with text, are not cut up.

**Tests.** The page checks on both backends that a change is built in more
than 3 slices with one build and is hit afterwards, and that a second
change that comes during the slices is in the result.
`test/unit/instructions.test.ts` has 2 new tests: instructions made in
slices equal those made in one go (and those of ol), and only what is asked
for is made. Unit 73 of 73, Rust 14 of 14, rendering 19 cases with 0
failures in every group, memory 0 failed.

**Measured.** The same page (WebGL2 / WebGPU):

| | Before | Now |
|---|---|---|
| One feature changed: built after | 215 / 234 ms | 221 / 251 ms |
| longest frame | 50 / 50 ms | 17 / 33 ms |
| long tasks | 1 (55 ms) / 1 (51 ms) | 0 / 0 |
| Another style: built after | 277 / 287 ms | 292 / 285 ms |
| longest frame | 117 / 117 ms | 17 / 17 ms |
| long tasks | 1 (115 ms) / 1 (123 ms) | 0 / 0 |

The work takes as long (9 slices for one feature, 21 for a style), up to 7%
longer until it is built; the page no longer waits for it. The benchmark is
as before: its `ready` is the same work with pauses in it.

**Not done.** Adding features to the layer's batch (ol's
`MixedGeometryBatch`, 455 ms for a million points) still happens in one go
when the features arrive. Uploading the buffers and loading the hit index
are not cut up either; they did not show as long tasks here.

## 10. `z-index` and `renderOrder`

**Was.** Features were drawn in the order they came into the source, per
style in the order of the styles, and within a style all fills, then all
strokes, then all symbols. `z-index` in a style did nothing (as in ol's
WebGL layers) and there was no `renderOrder`.

**Now.** As far as one buffer per geometry kind and one program per style
allow (`src/render/order.ts`):

- Styles with a number as `z-index` are drawn in the order of those
  numbers.
- A `z-index` that is an expression (`['get', 'priority']`) gives every
  feature a number: the features are in the buffers in that order, so a
  feature with a higher number is drawn over the others of its kind, and
  comes first in the hits. With rules it is the `z-index` of the first
  style whose filter the feature passes.
- `renderOrder`, a new layer option with `getRenderOrder()` and
  `setRenderOrder()`: a function that compares two features, as for ol's
  Canvas vector layer. It orders features with the same `z-index`.

Without either nothing changes: the order of the batch, and what ol draws.

**Tests.** A new page, `test/rendering/order.ts`, with three circles on one
spot per case, checks the colour on top and the order of the hits on both
backends: as they came; by `z-index`; by `renderOrder`; `z-index` before
`renderOrder`; rules with numbers; `setRenderOrder` and back; a feature
whose `z-index` changes. 7 of 7. `test/unit/order.test.ts` (new, 13 tests)
covers the order of styles, the number of a feature, and the order of the
instructions. Unit 86 of 86, Rust 14 of 14, rendering 19 cases with 0
failures in every group, memory 0 failed.

**Measured.** Without an order the benchmark is as before. With one, making
the point instructions of 500 000 features (in Node, twice: the first time
and again with the numbers kept):

| | First | Again |
|---|---|---|
| No order | 66 ms | 64 ms |
| `z-index` from a property, 1000 different numbers | 401 ms | 296 ms |
| `z-index` from a property, all different | 503 ms | 382 ms |
| `renderOrder` comparing a property | 2199 ms | 2059 ms |

The numbers are put in order with the sort of a typed array and a counting
pass, which is a small part of this; most of it is reading the features in
another order than they lie in memory. `renderOrder` calls the function
about 20 times per feature, and that is its cost: it is for layers of tens
of thousands of features, not millions. The order is made in one go at the
start of a build, not in slices.

**Not exact.** Still all fills, then all strokes, then all symbols per
style: a fill with a higher `z-index` does not cover the stroke of another
feature. A `z-index` that depends on variables or the zoom is taken when
the buffers are built; changing the variable alone builds nothing. With
`text: 'canvas'` (ol's generators) the features are not ordered. Labels
have their own order, by `text-z-index` as before.

## 11. Symbols in decluttering

**Was.** A layer with `declutter` placed its labels; its symbols (circles,
shapes, icons) were all drawn, whatever was under or over them.

**Now.** A symbol of a style with `circle-declutter-mode`,
`shape-declutter-mode` or `icon-declutter-mode` set to `'declutter'` or
`'obstacle'` is placed with the labels (`src/render/symbols.ts`):

- The symbol takes the space of its box (with displacement and anchor, as
  hit detection has it). With `'declutter'` it is not drawn where that
  space is taken, and not hit; with `'obstacle'` it is always drawn and
  keeps labels and other symbols away.
- A symbol and the label of its feature are one: both are drawn or neither.
- Features that came first win, as with labels; layers of one declutter
  group share the space from the top layer down.
- For points and multi points, with `text: 'gpu'` (the default).

Symbols that lost their place are left out of the point buffer: the
instances of the build are kept, and the ones to draw are uploaded again
when the set changes.

Unlike ol's Canvas layers a symbol without such a mode is always drawn
(there `'declutter'` is the default): layers that declutter their labels
and rely on their symbols keep them.

**Tests.** A new page, `test/rendering/symbols.ts`, 7 checks on both
backends: a symbol that overlaps an earlier one is not drawn and not hit;
symbols without a mode are all drawn; obstacles are all drawn and keep the
label of the layer below away; a symbol whose label has no place is not
drawn either; a symbol comes back after zooming in, goes again after
zooming out, and takes the place of one that is removed.
`test/unit/symbols.test.ts` (new, 4 tests) covers which symbols are placed
and their boxes. Unit 90 of 90, Rust 14 of 14, rendering 19 cases with 0
failures in every group, memory 0 failed.

**Measured.** 40 000 circles in the view (WebGL2 / WebGPU): shown after
87 / 70 ms when they are not placed, after 158 / 148 ms when they are (240
of them are drawn, 39 760 are not). The label times of layers without
placed symbols are as before. The benchmark was not run for this step (it
runs with item 12): without a mode in the style the only new work per frame
is one comparison.

**Not exact.** A symbol does not fade in or out as labels do. The box of a
symbol that turns (a rotation, or with the view) is the square around it.
With several worlds on screen a symbol is drawn in all of them when it has
a place in one. Symbols of lines and polygons (there are none in a flat
style) and `text: 'canvas'` are not covered.
